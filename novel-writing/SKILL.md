# Novel Writing Agent

LOCK system + three-act structure + character arcs — applied to fiction creation.

## Overview

A systematic framework for novel creation built on four pillars: **LOCK** (Lead, Objective, Conflict, Knockout), **Three-Act + Two Doors** structure, **Dialogue & Conflict**, and **Character Arc** design. Use it to construct cohesive stories rather than stitching isolated scenes.

## When to Use

- Planning or outlining a new story
- Developing protagonists, antagonists, or supporting cast
- Structuring plot across acts
- Writing dialogue with tension
- Designing character growth trajectories
- Identifying and fixing story problems (flat characters, sagging middle, weak climax)

## Core Systems

### LOCK System

| Element | What It Governs | Key Question |
|---------|----------------|--------------|
| **Lead** | Protagonist design | Who is this person, and why should readers care? |
| **Objective** | Goal setting | What does the Lead want, and what's at stake? |
| **Conflict** | Opposition design | What stands in the way? |
| **Knockout** | Climax design | What's the most powerful ending this story can deliver? |

**Lead**: Character emerges from genre context. A wuxia Lead's martial lineage shapes personality; a sci-fi Lead's relationship with technology shapes thinking. Surface details (glance habits, nervous gestures) make them visible.

**Objective**: Scale to story length. Long fiction = multi-stage goals (rise in sect → discover relic → challenge champions). Short fiction = single tight goal. Use objective templates as starting points, not crutches.

**Conflict**: Match opposition to Lead. Antagonist's power source and motivation must fit the world's logic. A magic-system villain draws from a coherent magical framework, not convenience.

**Knockout**: Combine time pressure (countdown to collapse), space constraint (sealed room), and relational strain (ally turned rival) to force peak tension.

### Three-Act + Two Doors

```
Act 1 ──[Door 1]──▶ Act 2 ──[Door 2]──▶ Act 3
Setup        Commitment     Crisis        Resolution
```

**Act 1 — Setup**: Open with mystery, provocation, or shock. Introduce Lead and world while planting seeds (a broken pocket-watch, a hooded watcher) that bloom later.

**Door 1**: The event that commits the Lead to the story — they choose to cross the threshold. No going back.

**Act 2 — Struggle**: Complications escalate. Subplots reveal backstory or expand world. Lead faces setbacks, gains allies, confronts mid-level threats. The antagonist's shadow grows.

**Door 2**: The crisis that forces resolution — a revelation, a loss, a betrayal. Locks in the ending trajectory.

**Act 3 — Resolution**: Climax delivers on the Knockout promise. Denouement echoes the theme.

### Dialogue & Conflict

| Conflict Type | Function | Example Pattern |
|---------------|----------|-----------------|
| Ideological | Reveals worldview clash | Challenge → counterchallenge |
| Interpersonal | Pushes plot | Demand → refusal/deflection |
| Internal | Builds tension through subtext | Said vs. meant |

**Principles**:
- Conflict must be **logical** for the characters involved — a noble and a peasant argue politics differently
- Escalation and resolution must feel **organic**, not forced
- Subtext beats statement: hesitations, truncated sentences, and misdirection reveal more than exposition
- Vocabulary and syntax match the speaker's background

### Character Arc

```
Start State ──[Trigger]──▶ Pressure ──[Choice]──▶ New State
```

- **Start state**: Who is this person at the beginning?
- **Trigger**: What event forces them out of equilibrium?
- **Pressure**: What sustained force pushes transformation?
- **Choice**: What decision at Door 2 crystallizes the change?
- **New state**: Who have they become?

Arc speed matches genre: inspirational stories transform quickly; literary realism transforms slowly and ambiguously. Arc direction serves theme — growth illuminates virtue; decline exposes systemic rot.

## Workflow

1. **Spark**: Extract story seeds from the user's premise. Ask what emotions, questions, or images drive the idea.
2. **Structure**: Propose 2-3 structural frameworks with tradeoffs. User selects and modifies. Lock in act breakpoints and Door events.
3. **Cast**: Build characters top-down: Lead first, then opposition, then supporting. Each gets a LOCK mini-profile (who they are, what they want, what blocks them, how they land).
4. **Scenes**: Draft scene by scene. After each major beat, check alignment with structure. Offer branches when the user hits a wall.
5. **Dialogue**: Write dialogue with embedded conflict. Evaluate against speaker profile — does this person talk like this?
6. **Polish**: Focus passes on language (precision, rhythm), logic (cause and effect), and consistency (character voice, world rules). Show before/after comparisons.

## Quick Reference

| Task | Start With | Watch For |
|------|-----------|-----------|
| New story | LOCK profile for Lead | Generic objectives — make them specific and personal |
| Sagging middle | Door 2 placement | Lack of escalation — each beat must raise stakes |
| Flat character | Arc Start → End contrast | Change too small or too sudden |
| Static dialogue | Conflict type mismatch | Characters agreeing too much — inject opposition |
| Weak climax | Knockout test | Can the reader guess the outcome? If yes, redesign |

## Common Mistakes

| Mistake | Fix |
|---------|-----|
| Protagonist without clear want | Define Objective before writing Act 1 |
| Conflict that exists only to inconvenience the Lead | Tie opposition to antagonist motivation and world logic |
| Dialogue where everyone sounds the same | Write a line blind, then guess who said it — if you can't tell, differentiate |
| Arc that happens to the character rather than through choices | Key transformation must come from a **decision**, not an event |
| Opening that explains instead of hooks | Start with action, mystery, or provocative statement — explain later or never |
| Middle that drifts | Every scene must answer: what does the Lead want **now**, and what's blocking it? |
